What you have just read, and what it does not settle

The summaries gave you an account of thirty-four chapters: the constitutional claim, the mechanism said to have cancelled it, the standing analysis, both prongs, the remedy, and the objections. What they cannot give you is verification. A summary is a claim about a chapter, and a chapter is a claim about the authorities beneath it. Everything on this page exists so that you can go down a level and check.

Three things in particular remain unproven and are not hidden. No court has ruled on the question, so the central claim is an argument rather than a holding. The platform-year standards anchor — that a certified remake should answer to the standards in force when its original was certified — is the argument’s own inference, flagged as such wherever it appears and nowhere presented as something a court has said. And the cost estimates are estimates, built from a stated methodology that is set out rather than asserted.

If you only do one more thing: read Chapter One, “Four Problems, One Remedy” in full. It is the whole argument in the author’s own words, in ordinary language, in about twenty-five minutes — and it is the fastest way to find out whether the summaries flattered it.

1. Test the argument yourself

One chapter is built precisely for a reader who does not want to be told. It hands you the primary materials — thirty-four Supreme Court holdings with operative language, the regulatory provisions quoted verbatim, the statutory authority, and both advocates’ cases stated fairly — and invites you to put the question to an artificial-intelligence model and judge the reasoning for yourself. Eleven independent models, reasoning from those materials, converged on a finding that a cognizable injury exists. They decide nothing; only a court can. The convergence is offered as one more thing you can weigh and verify without taking anyone’s word for it. What makes the file worth an hour is the file, not the results: it is a research memorandum whatever you conclude from it.

  • The examination materials

    The exam as a PDF or Word file, the scoring sheet, and step-by-step instructions for running it yourself. Start here if you want to test rather than read.

  • The full examination chapter

    The holdings, the diagrams, the standards tables, the seven prompts, and the scoring, reproduced in full with live links throughout.

  • Appendix C — the eleven models’ complete replies

    Each model’s full, unedited reasoning, with a link to its own session. The record is dated and closed; it reports a run that happened rather than a claim about models in general.

2. Check the sources

  • Appendix A — the SCOTUS Case Master Table

    Every Supreme Court case cited anywhere in the two volumes, sorted by decision year, each described for what it actually holds and scored by the vote. Inclusion is not endorsement: the cases that cut against the argument are in there too, and the entry says so.

  • The complete endnotes, glossary, and key terms

    The full citation apparatus, prepared to Chicago 17th edition, with a working source link at the end of every entry. Searchable by find-in-page. This is where a claim gets traced to its document in a single tap.

The discipline the apparatus is built to is worth stating plainly, because it is the thing an attorney will check first: cases are cited only for what they hold, rationale is never presented as holding, and every inference the argument makes beyond its authorities is flagged in the text as the argument’s own.

3. Read the argument in the author’s own words

  • Chapter One — “Four Problems, One Remedy”

    The opening chapter in full. What the Patent Clause promised, what happened to that promise after 1970, and why one federal courtroom can answer it. Roughly twenty-five minutes.

  • When Outsiders Advance Constitutional Law and Other Disciplines

    Twenty cases in which someone outside a field saw what everyone inside it had missed. The chapter written for the reader whose objection is the author rather than the argument.

  • The Two-Volume Brief

    What each volume contains, the chapter structure, the constitutional argument in outline, and how the two books carry it between them.

  • The abbreviated preface

    The shortest honest account of who wrote this, how it was built, and what was and was not done by machine.
